# Security Review: session-timeout-continuity design

**Reviewer:** Tomás Ferreira, Senior Application Security Analyst
**Scope reviewed:** design.md, proposal.md, and delta specs `websocket-session-authorization`, `websocket-staleness-signal`, `reauth-return-to`, `facilitator-reconnect-indicator`. Cross-checked against current implementation: `packages/backend/src/realtime/websocket-routes.ts`, `connection-reauthorization.ts`, `packages/backend/src/routes/auth.ts`, `packages/frontend/src/realtime/connectionHealth.ts`, `packages/shared/src/types/ws-close-codes.ts`.

## Summary

Both narrowings are defensible and each is documented with an explicit rationale in the delta specs rather than left implicit — that alone puts this ahead of most "we relaxed a security requirement" changes I review. I have no blocking findings. I have one finding I want closed before implementation (silent discard of a probe-shaped input with no audit trail), and several non-blocking items I want tracked in tasks.md.

## Constraint 1 — Close-code disclosure-symmetry break

**Verdict: safe, with one edge case worth naming explicitly in the spec.**

I traced the actual close paths in the current code, not just the design's description of them:

- Rejected-subscription-at-connect-time closes: `STALE_SIGNAL_CLOSE_CODE` (`websocket-routes.ts:88`). Unchanged.
- SEC-25 periodic re-authorization sweep (revocation discovered on a live connection, every 5 minutes): `STALE_SIGNAL_CLOSE_CODE` (`connection-reauthorization.ts:104`). Unchanged, and this is the mechanism that actually force-closes a *live* revoked connection — I want that on the record, since the design's Context section discusses revocation mostly in terms of the delivery-time "silent withholding" behavior, which is a different, non-closing mechanism. The sweep is the one that closes, and it stays disclosure-blind.
- Absolute-lifetime force-close (`scheduleForceClose`): today `STALE_SIGNAL_CLOSE_CODE`, changing to `REAUTH_GRACE_EXPIRED_CLOSE_CODE`.

Given that, the question is whether an attacker who can trigger or observe a forced disconnect learns anything new. They cannot, for two independent reasons:

1. **No cross-user channel exists.** A WebSocket close code is only visible to the client whose own socket was closed. There is no path for User B to observe User A's close code. So "distinguishing timed-out from revoked" is only ever information about *your own* connection, never a way to fingerprint someone else's session.
2. **The disclosed code never asserts "not revoked."** `REAUTH_GRACE_EXPIRED_CLOSE_CODE` means "your absolute lifetime elapsed or your silent refresh failed" — full stop. It says nothing about whether you were *also* separately revoked. If a user is revoked and coincidentally also crosses the 90-minute mark within the same ~5-minute sweep window, they may get the disclosed code before the sweep catches the revocation. They then follow the reauth CTA, re-authenticate successfully against the IdP (revocation here is app-side team/role membership, not IdP-side), and land back on the destination route via `returnTo` — where `reauth-return-to`'s own "does not grant, infer, or bypass any authorization" requirement means the destination's normal authz check denies them there, exactly as a direct navigation would. The denial is real and happens at the right layer; it's just delivered one hop later than it would be if the sweep had won the race. That's a timing artifact, not a disclosure regression — but I want it named as an explicit edge case in the `websocket-session-authorization` delta spec (a scenario under the "distinguishable" requirement), not left for an implementer to reconstruct from two files six months from now the way I just had to.

I also checked the new `facilitator-reconnect-indicator` capability against this same question, since it's new wire surface that didn't exist before this change and it's the one place cause-blindness is asserted as a *requirement* rather than inherited from existing code. The payload design (`{ connected: boolean }`, no cause) is correct and sufficient at the payload level. But cause-blindness at the payload level does not guarantee cause-blindness in the *emergent* signal: a facilitator who is gone for 30 seconds and a facilitator who is gone permanently (role removed, access revoked, employment ended) produce the identical payload shape, but participants will still be able to distinguish "blip" from "permanent" purely by *how long* the indicator persists — duration is itself a side channel the spec doesn't acknowledge. Practically this is low-severity (it discloses that someone is gone, not why), but it interacts with a real product gap: there's no requirement anywhere in this spec for the indicator to ever stop showing "reconnecting" if the facilitator's disconnect turns out to be permanent (revoked, removed from team). As written, participants could sit looking at "Facilitator reconnecting…" indefinitely. I'd like tasks.md to at least name this as a known limitation rather than something that surfaces as a support ticket and gets "fixed" ad hoc later.

## Constraint 2 — `returnTo` allow-list and open-redirect / session-fixation risk

**Verdict: the mechanism design is sound; one gap needs closing before implementation, one needs the spec to be more precise than it currently is.**

What's right:

- The value sent to the IdP as `state` is a random 32-byte token (`auth.ts:85`); `returnTo` never leaves this app's own Redis-backed payload and is never visible to, or round-tripped through, the IdP. I verified this against the actual code, not just the design's claim — `getAuthorizationUrl` receives the opaque token, not the JSON blob. This is genuinely provider-agnostic: nothing about the mechanism depends on which OIDC provider is configured, and it doesn't touch a claim, so [[project_oidc_multi_provider]]'s constraint (Entra is primary, not exclusive) holds.
- The final redirect is always constructed as `${appOrigin}${redirectUrl}` (`auth.ts:337`), where `appOrigin` is server-controlled and `redirectUrl` is required to be a bare allow-listed path. That composition is what actually prevents an off-origin redirect — not the `://`/leading-`//` blocklist check in isolation, which is defense-in-depth on top of it.
- "Return-to preservation does not grant, infer, or bypass any authorization" is stated as an explicit requirement with a scenario, not assumed. This is exactly the right response to the obvious attack of sending a victim a crafted `returnTo=/team/<id-they-aren't-a-member-of>` link — the destination's own authz check denies it. I don't see a session-fixation angle here at all: `session.regenerate()` already runs unconditionally on every callback (`auth.ts:262`) before any redirect decision, so `returnTo` has no interaction with session identity.

**Blocking-before-implementation finding:** the allow-list rejection path is specified as fully silent — "discarded silently... without surfacing an error to the user" (reauth-return-to spec, Requirement 2) — and the design gives no audit signal for a rejected value either. Compare this to the *existing* `loginHint` validation four lines above it in the same file, which returns a 400 and is a validated rejection path. A `returnTo` value that fails the allow-list is exactly the shape of thing someone testing for an open redirect would send, and today's design makes that attempt invisible to any log or audit trail — it fails closed, correctly, but leaves no trace that it happened. Auditability is a security control, not a nicety: I want a rejected/discarded `returnTo` to at minimum emit a debug-level structured log (not necessarily `emitAuditEvent`'s audit tier, since this isn't a successful auth event — a `request.log.debug` or `warn` is enough) so repeated probing is visible to anyone who goes looking. This is a small addition to task 2.2/2.3 in tasks.md, not a design change.

**Needs more precision, not a redesign:** the allow-list's actual security value rests entirely on how tightly the `:id` segment is constrained, and neither design.md nor the delta spec pins that down — "known internal path shapes (`/session/:id`, `/team/:id`)" is stated by example, not by pattern. If the implementation matches `:id` against a loose class (anything up to the next `?` or end-of-string, say), that's almost certainly fine given IDs in this system are UUIDs, but I want the spec or tasks.md to say so explicitly — e.g. "`:id` matches this application's UUID id format, rejecting anything else" — rather than leaving the exact regex to be decided silently at implementation time with no spec to hold it to. This is the same category of thing the design.md Risks section already flags for the allow-list drifting from `executeJoinFlow`'s shapes over time; I'm asking for the same rigor applied to the *shape* of each entry, not just the *set* of entries. Concretely: reject anything containing a CR or LF (header-injection into the eventual `Location` header — I didn't find an existing guard against this anywhere in the redirect-construction path, allow-listed or not, and it costs nothing to state), and reject backslash characters (`\`) in addition to the stated `://`/leading-`//` checks, since some URL-parsing contexts treat `\` as `/` and a strict allow-list match should already exclude it, but I'd rather it be named than relied on implicitly.

Everything else in Decision 3 — single-use via the existing atomic `getdel`, precedence of `pendingJoinToken` over `returnTo`, no new Redis-side risk since it rides the existing TTL'd payload — is sound and I have no notes.

## Audit logging impact

This is the one area where the design is thinner than the rest, and it's worth being explicit about why, since it's my primary lens on this project. The good news, which the design doesn't call out but should: facilitator connect/disconnect for *any* cause is **already** written to `audit_log` today, unconditionally and cause-blind, at the exact call sites (`websocket-routes.ts:137`, `:158`) this change's new `facilitator_connection_status` broadcast needs to hook into (issue #94's `session.facilitator_connected`/`disconnected` rows). That existing mechanism already gives Priya's and my shared concern — "does the audit trail see facilitator connection health with the same fidelity as HTTP events" — a real answer: yes, independent of this change.

What I want stated, not assumed: tasks.md's 4.1/4.2 should say explicitly that the new broadcast is wired at those same two call sites (not a second, independently-derived detection of "facilitator disconnected"), so the client-facing signal and the audit-log signal can never drift out of sync with each other. As drafted, task 4.2 ("wire this broadcast to fire on facilitator disconnect... and reconnect") is satisfiable by a second, parallel piece of detection logic that happens to agree with the first today and silently diverges later. One call site, two consumers (the audit write, the broadcast) — not two call sites that happen to agree.

## Other notes (non-blocking)

- Decision 1/4's `MODIFIED` deltas are each written with the narrow "what changed and why" framing the design's own Risks section promises — I checked, and they hold up; a future reader who only reads the archived spec text (not this design doc) would not mistake either narrowing for a general erosion.
- I confirmed `websocket-staleness-signal`'s D9 single-component guarantee is respected by the role/`returnTo` prop additions — both are explicit params supplied by the caller, not inferred from ambient state, matching the pattern the rest of that module already uses.
- No deferred or implicit security decision in this design escaped without a stated rationale, with the two exceptions above (silent-discard audit gap, indicator persistence on permanent facilitator loss) — both are now named rather than left for someone to notice in production.

## Disposition

Not blocking on the two narrowings themselves — both are correct, bounded, and legible from the spec text alone. Blocking on: adding a log signal for rejected `returnTo` values before this ships (small, task-list-level fix). Requesting, not blocking on: pinning the `:id` pattern precisely in tasks.md, naming the CRLF/backslash checks explicitly, wiring the new broadcast to the existing audit call sites rather than a parallel detection path, and naming the indefinite-indicator-persistence limitation somewhere in tasks.md so it's a known trade-off rather than a future surprise.
